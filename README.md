> **The difference between existing bots and always-on agents begins with what promise the service takes on.**

In the same chat window, one fan wants to know when a concert starts, another wants to talk with a favorite character, and a third wants someone to wait for a concert that has not been announced yet and check whether it fits their schedule and budget. The first two requests are fulfilled within the conversation. The third leaves work that must survive after the conversation ends.

This report separates **promises kept within the conversation at hand** from **promises that remain after it ends**, and sets out the conditions of **memory, authority, and official status** needed to keep them. In a market where general-purpose AI can already deliver conversation and automation, it asks what an entertainment company can still add.

## Three Functions of an Entertainment Bot

An entertainment bot is described as software with a role connected to fans, artists, characters, or organizations, that:

| Function | Examples |
|---|---|
| ① **Creates experiences** | talking with a character, developing impressions and creative work |
| ② **Carries context forward** | drawing on tastes and earlier conversations, continuing requests in progress |
| ③ **Performs activities** | checking official information, handling permitted applications and tasks |

These are not stages of development — each bot combines what its role needs. Three questions are decided together at design time: **Whose role is it? What does it remember? How far can it act?**

## What "Always On" Really Means

"Always on" bundles three different things:

- **Availability** — you can connect and ask at any time
- **Persistence** — a delegated task and its state remain in place
- **Triggering** — work starts on a set time or an external change, without being asked again

A service can be reachable 24 hours a day and still hold nothing to do later. So three independent questions must be asked of any agent: **How long does the delegated goal last? What makes the next piece of work start? How far can that work act?** Bundling "remember," "start first," and "act on someone's behalf" into one description inflates expectations.

The user must also be able to see when a promised task has stalled — *still checking*, *access expired*, *waiting for human approval*, *done*.

## Four Kinds of Bots, Four Different Masters

| Bot | Works for | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Fan's personal bot** | the fan | draws on the fan's tastes, schedule, and budget |
| **Artist / character bot** | the experience | conversation within approved settings |
| **Company's official bot** | the operator | public information and procedures |
| **Internal work bot** | managers | tracks requests, schedule changes, approvals |

Their interests are not always aligned: a fan may want to spend less while a company wants more participation. If advertising or partnership terms influence recommendations, the fan must be able to see those criteria.

## Whose Words Are These?

Even inside one official service, three kinds of statement can sit side by side — a **message written by the artist**, **guidance confirmed by the operations team**, and a **response generated by the bot**. The fact that an artist approved a bot's settings is not the same as the artist having written an individual reply. "Official service" describes an operating relationship; it does not mean the artist wrote, or promised, every sentence.

## Memory, Relationships, and Continuity

**Memory is managed by kind:** official knowledge (kept current by the operator), the fan's personal context (which the fan can check, correct, and delete), and tasks in progress (linked to real results). Before memory is used, three checks apply: *Is it needed now? Is it still right? May it be used for this purpose?*

**Three relationships coexist in one conversation:** fan and artist (the one the fan loves), fan and bot (what produces the reply now), fan and company (who keeps the records). A bot drawing on a fan's earlier words does not mean the real artist read them.

**When the app changes,** three things must be checked separately: preserving records, moving functions, and continuing the experience. Exporting files does not guarantee the same experience.

## When My Bot Contacts Someone Else's Bot

A fan's bot asking an official bot about concerts should pass **only the conditions the request needs** — dates and region, not the fan's whole conversation history. Protocols such as A2A make agent cooperation technically possible, but a protocol alone creates neither authority nor an agreement between companies.

**Recommending is not booking.** Finding a concert means gathering material for a choice; buying a ticket is a real transaction with money, seats, and cancellation terms. Delegated booking needs specific conditions, re-approval when they are exceeded, and completion confirmed by an actual booking number and payment record — not by the bot saying "I've booked it." If a response is cut off after a payment request, check the existing request before paying again.

## What Entertainment Companies Can Add

Natural conversation and basic automation are now available from general-purpose AI. What an entertainment business can offer beyond that:

1. **Accuracy of official information**
2. **The scope within which artists and characters can be used**
3. **Connection to actual work**
4. **A sender label fans can understand**

None of these is secured merely by changing the model. Official status itself must be verifiable — who operates the bot, which names and materials it may use, what experience it has been approved to provide — and renewed or ended when contracts change. When a bot says or promises something wrong, the developer, the operating company, the staff who supplied information, and the requesting user each have a distinct part in setting it right.
